## 00:00:00–00:01:43  [talking-head]
**Spoken:** (continues from 150 — the presenter, Josh Kim, keeps answering) Audience: "…human in the loop. What would you advise — completely automate those tasks and let it run, or where are the limits and constraints when it comes to the human loop?" Presenter: "Great question — it's come up in a lot of customer conversations. I lean on the analogy of treating a bot like a teammate: like onboarding someone early in their career, you don't give them the keys to the kingdom immediately. Give them quick context, train them up, onboard them — it happens much faster with these. Start gradually: give a simple task, hook up one or two tools where they'll get context quickly, see that they're working and delivering value, and getting better over time. As you keep prompting and giving feedback and curating it to how you work and what you care about, it takes more tasks off your plate and becomes truly automated. There are also settings where you can prompt it — 'hey, you asked me for permission to do these things?' — and 'always allow' if you want to be more liberal. A good end state: engineers at SpaceX AI have entire armies of bots working and pushing code to prod. You get there with rich prompting, memory, and context — but start small, low-risk, and go gradual."

**Actions:** Presenter answers the human-in-the-loop question.
**Facts:**
- Recommended pattern: treat a bot like a new teammate — onboard gradually (simple task → one or two tools → feedback), not full autonomy at once
- There is an "always allow" permission setting for users who want to be more liberal with approvals
- Cited end state: SpaceX AI engineers run "armies of bots" that push code to production, reached via rich prompting + memory + context

## 00:01:43–00:03:19  [talking-head]
**Spoken:** Audience: "What's the biggest blocker for marketers using this technology?" Presenter: "New users often come in without a clear understanding of what's actually capable — that's why I spent time up front saying 'this is different.' You need to shift your mental model away from treating it as a Google/AI search or another LLM where you ask questions and get answers. That's not how it works — you have to trust it to actually do things for you. A common team pattern: hook it into Slack, and it becomes the first line of defense — triage; it receives questions in Slack/DMs/email, messages you when something's important or urgent, and messages back the person responding. So it won't be valuable if you treat it as another LLM; it'll be extremely valuable if you say 'you can do things for me,' test it, throw tasks at it, give it context, and see what it's capable of."
**On screen:** Presenter on camera.
**Actions:** Presenter answers the "biggest blocker" question.
**Facts:**
- Biggest blocker: users treating Grok Bot as an LLM/search (Q&A) instead of trusting it to do work
- Reference pattern: a bot hooked into Slack acts as first-line triage — flags important/urgent items to the human and replies to the requester
