## 00:06:20–00:09:35  [talking-head]
**Spoken:** Audience Q: "If the bots just keep evolving — what do you think would be the martech stack that's going to disappear first? And how do you think about the relationship between bots and humans?" Matthew: "Yeah. I think of it — I really think of it a lot as a **mirror**. Oftentimes, when I'm talking to bots, especially in one of those thought-partnership sessions like you saw with the Q&A, I think of the bot as — of course, in some ways it's influencing how I think about what I'm trying to do. So like, it might bring up certain aspects of the build for that app that I didn't think of myself. But ultimately it's then prompting me to do that thinking. And I'm like, 'huh, okay, yeah, good point. And now I'm thinking about it. Now I'm really engaged with it.' And now my engagement with that thought breeds more thoughts and more ideas, and then I just share that with a bot, and then it has more context to work off of. And so you can think of it as, like, if nothing else, you're sort of like thinking out loud with these bots — and they're giving you — you know, in some ways we prompt AI, in some ways AI is prompting us to think more deeply about what we're trying to accomplish with it. So that's what I think about the relationship between me and the bot, or anyone who uses Grok Bot. In terms of the stack that I think will go away, it's sort of hard to say. I think there will probably still be many instances of — like, it's really good to just understand the right button to click and menus to navigate to use certain complex tools. But I think something that's sort of happened, especially with coding, is like the barrier to entry is so much lower. I think the engineering profession — there's this sort of reckoning over the past few years that more and more professions are going to start to have with the introduction of these kind of tools, around like, 'okay, what do I do if my role is no longer just being the gatekeeper of this really complicated looking code that no one else understands?' Like now all of a sudden anyone can produce that code. And so what's the value add of an engineer? There is a value add. There definitely is. The value add is more about that higher-level thinking, that intuitive feel for the systems. And so I think it's helpful to really get to know the tools deeply, because of — similar to before — what it prompts you to think about. No one really wants to be [an expert] at navigating menus, but there's something about that tedious work that really makes you slow down and think about what you're trying to do in those complex tools — just like AI also prompts you to slow down and think. So I think that is what's going to remain. I think using complex tools to the extent that it gives you deep familiarity with your profession, and expertise and taste — I think that will persist. But I also think that in many ways there will be a lot less button clicking and like wiring things up properly in setting up the integrations. So a little bit vague, but hopefully you sort of understand philosophically what I mean."
